A Cup Before Class

At 8:10 on a Monday morning, the line at Copper Cup Coffee reaches the door. Students check their phones and order before class. One asks which drink is cheapest. Another notices a sign beside the register: Coming soon: The Story of the Bean.

Copper Cup is a small, independent cafe near an Arizona university campus. Owner Elena Torres wants to introduce a featured coffee whose journey customers can follow from a farmer cooperative to the counter. She wants the story to be interesting, but it also needs to be accurate.

Elena asks Maya Alvarez, a first-year W. P. Carey student who works part time at the cafe, to help. Maya expects to write a menu description. Instead, Elena places an unroasted coffee sample, a supplier email, and a draft budget on the table.

“Before we tell the story,” Elena says, “we need to understand it.”

Maya starts with a simple question: How does a coffee bean get from a farm to our shop? Answering it will take more than knowing how to make a latte.

Copper Cup, its characters, and its named business partners are fictional. Their story gives you a setting in which to explore real business concepts.

Eight Ways to See the Same Business

The Story of the Bean connects eight W. P. Carey disciplines. These examples introduce the kinds of questions each discipline asks. Your course modules provide the specific activities and evidence.

Agribusiness and Food Industry Management

The bean begins as a crop. Weather, farm labor, resources, and relationships affect the coffee long before it reaches the menu. This perspective connects the food system with what the cafe can offer customers.

Consider: Which sourcing option fits Copper Cup's needs, and what can it honestly say about that coffee?

Economics

Coffee is bought and sold in a market with competing sellers. Economics helps explain how changing costs, customer interest, and limited resources influence prices and choices.

Consider: What might happen to market price and quantity when supply decreases and demand increases?

Finance

A new system costs money now, while its benefits may arrive later. Finance helps Copper Cup compare future benefits, risk, and ways to pay while keeping enough cash for everyday needs.

Consider: Is an investment worth its cost, and how could the cafe fund it?

Management and Entrepreneurship

Someone has to turn the idea into workable tasks. Management connects planning, staffing, leadership, and feedback. Entrepreneurship helps the team test an idea and learn before making a larger commitment.

Consider: Who should carry out the work, and what support will that person need?

Marketing

An interesting origin story is useful only if it connects with customers. Marketing examines customer needs, the value of an offering, and how to communicate that value accurately.

Consider: Who is this coffee for, and which message would be meaningful and credible?

Supply Chain Management

The right coffee needs to arrive at the right place and time. Supply chain management connects sourcing, transportation, inventory, and information across partners, including when a disruption changes the plan.

Consider: How should Copper Cup respond when a delivery is delayed?

Accountancy

Purchases, sales, and other business events leave a financial record. Accountancy helps Copper Cup organize those records and communicate what they show about resources and performance.

Consider: What do the cafe's records tell us about the featured coffee's financial results?

Information Systems

Records are useful only when people can rely on and use them. Information Systems connects people, business processes, and technology to organize, analyze, share, and protect information.

Consider: What information does Copper Cup need, who should access it, and how should it be protected?

Look Beyond the Cafe

A business choice can affect people who are not at the counter: farmers, employees, customers, partners, lenders, and communities. These are stakeholders, people or groups affected by a business and its decisions.

A lower price may help customers but leave less money to cover costs. A longer explanation may help customers understand the coffee but add to employees' workload. Notice who benefits, who bears a cost or risk, and what evidence would help Copper Cup decide. You are not expected to eliminate every tradeoff.
